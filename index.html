7.Here are some of my favorite books

1 of 5

A Dance with Dragons

George R. R. Martin

Fans of the series will insist that ADWD is not the best book in the series. They will point to the action of A Storm of Swords, the themes explored in A Feast for Crows, the iconic A Game of Thrones or the characters in A Clash of Kings, but I disagree. There is a reason The Winds of Winter is the most anticipated book release of all time, and that reason is the fiery excitement ADWD inspires in readers for the future of the series. It is all starting to come together in ADWD, and the book arguably contains the most politicking and intrigue of any of the five, and whenever I give it a reread I am buzzing just as much as the first time I read it.

The (Mis)Behavior of Markets

Benoit Mandelbrot and Richard L. Hudson

This book was one of my first introductions to what I would describe as the science behind financial markets. Mandelbrot (yes, that Mandelbrot) argues that “once in a generation market events” are not aptly named, and are much more prevalent than Gaussian traditionalists would have us believe. Because markets have memory, and price charts are fractal, a calm historical period can greatly underestimate future volatility. His reasoning method for understanding the market helped spark my interest in CS and stats, and has made me into a programmer who is skeptical of backtests and approaches market events with an open mind.

Oathbringer

Brandon Sanderson

Oathbringer is a triumph. The Stormlight Archive is a series about overcoming trauma, and how much people can grow when they do. Dalinar Kholin’s past, while laugh-out-loud funny at times, gives you a look into the troubled backstory of a character that up to that point we see as the rock of the Kholin family. We see the origin of his great strength, the fate of his first wife, and the B-plot trek through Shadesmar also gives us a look into the fallout of the Recreance on the spren, important setup for later in the series. The famous Sanderlanche at the end of Oathbringer embodies that, and is one of the only times I have audibly cheered when reading a book.

The Art of Impossible

Steven Kotler

The only self-help/productivity book I would recommend beyond Atomic Habits. I first stumbled across a video of Kotler talking about motivation on the Big Think YouTube channel, and he sold me on his book, and I am so glad I bought it. Flow state is a powerful tool if you know how to wield it, and entering it to complete marathon study and coding sessions is one of my biggest strengths. The book is less about feeling and more about directing and doing. It really spoke to me and is something I would recommend to anyone struggling with focus or endurance in what they do.

Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas

Hunter S. Thompson

Hunter S. Thompson is a writer like no other. His expertly crafted sentences will draw you into the story and make you feel like you are cruising through the Nevada desert in a convertible listening to The Rolling Stones. Beyond the style, Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas tells a story that is sadder than it appears on the surface, and will spark introspection in anyone who reads it. The death of the ’60s is an inflection point many see as the end of something carefree and wonderful in this country, and Thompson underscores that theme in a way only he can. The book’s wacky surrealism will leave you with more questions than answers, but you will know you just read something great.
